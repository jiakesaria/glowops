# Source Summaries: Crisis Escalation, Signaling, and Audience Costs

Ten papers on why international disputes escalate, how countries show they are serious, and whether leaders pay a price at home for backing down.

### Key terms

- **Escalation:** a dispute getting more serious, from threats to troop movements to fighting to war.
- **Audience costs:** the political price a leader pays at home if they make a public threat and then back down.
- **Costly signal:** an action that is expensive or risky, so a country that isn't serious wouldn't bother doing it. That's what makes it believable.
- **Game theory model:** a math model of how two sides make choices when each one's best move depends on what the other does. It uses logic, not real-world data.
- **Militarized Interstate Disputes (MIDs):** a widely used dataset that records every time one country threatened, showed, or used force against another.

### Types of study explained

- **Peer reviewed:** Before publication, other experts in the field read the paper, checked its methods, and asked for fixes. A journal publishes it only after it passes that review. This doesn't mean the paper is right, but it has been checked.
- **Game theory model:** The author describes a situation with math, for example two countries in a standoff, each choosing to push harder or back down. Then they work out what a sensible player would do. The result is a set of predictions. No real-world data is used, so the conclusions are only as good as the assumptions the model starts with.
- **Theory paper:** The author builds an argument step by step with logic and examples, sometimes backed by simple math. The goal is to explain *why* something happens, not to test it with data.
- **Statistical analysis:** The author gathers data on many real events, often hundreds or thousands of disputes, and uses math to look for patterns. For example: do disputes over land turn violent more often than other disputes? The strength is the large number of cases. The weakness is that it shows what tends to go together, which isn't always the same as what causes what.
- **Lab experiments:** Volunteers are brought into a controlled setting and given a task, here a simulated standoff between countries. The researcher randomly decides who gets which conditions and compares how the groups behave. Random assignment makes it easier to prove cause and effect. The downside is that students in a lab are not world leaders in a real crisis.
- **Review of case studies (quasi-meta-analysis):** The author doesn't collect new data. Instead, they gather detailed studies of individual historical events that other researchers wrote, then judge how much each one supports or undercuts a theory. Putting them together gives a bigger-picture verdict than any single case can.

## 1. Braithwaite & Lemke (2011), "Unpacking Escalation"

- **Published in:** *Conflict Management and Peace Science*, Vol. 28, No. 2
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Statistical analysis of historical data (MIDs)

**What it's about:** Researchers measure "escalation" in many different ways. This paper asks whether that matters.

**What they did:** Took six common ways of measuring escalation and checked whether well-known findings held up under each one.

**Main finding:** Most findings depend on which measure you pick and don't hold up across all six. The one exception is **territory**: disputes over land consistently escalate more, no matter how you measure it.

**Why it matters:** It's a warning that results in this field can come from how researchers define their terms, not from what actually happened.

## 2. Carlson (1995), "A Theory of Escalation and International Conflict"

- **Published in:** *Journal of Conflict Resolution*, Vol. 39, No. 3
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Game theory model, then a statistical test on MID data

**What it's about:** Most escalation research focuses on one setting, like arms races or deterrence. Carlson tries to build a general theory that works across settings.

**What she did:** Built a two-player model where each side doesn't know how much pain the other can take. Then she tested one prediction from it against real disputes.

**Main finding:** The bigger the gap in how much cost each side can tolerate, the more likely the *weaker-stomached* side is to escalate as hard as it can right at the start. The idea is that it tries to win quickly before a long fight wears it down. The data supported this.

## 3. Fearon (1994), "Domestic Political Audiences and the Escalation of International Disputes"

- **Published in:** *American Political Science Review*, Vol. 88, No. 3
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Game theory model (no real-world data)

**What it's about:** This is the paper that started the "audience costs" idea. It's one of the most cited papers in the field.

**The core idea:** A crisis is like a public game of chicken. The longer a leader stays in a public standoff, the more embarrassing it becomes to back down, because voters and rivals at home are watching.

**Main findings:**
- That growing embarrassment is useful. It lets each side see how serious the other really is.
- Once a crisis starts, the side that can suffer bigger audience costs is less likely to back down. Military strength matters less than you'd expect.
- Democracies should have an edge here because their leaders are more exposed to public punishment. That makes their threats more believable.

## 4. Fearon (1995), "Rationalist Explanations for War"

- **Published in:** *International Organization*, Vol. 49, No. 3
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Theory paper (logical argument backed by simple bargaining models)

**What it's about:** War is costly for both sides. So why don't countries just agree on a deal that both would prefer to fighting?

**Main argument:** Even fully rational leaders can end up at war for three reasons:
1. **Hidden information and bluffing.** Each side knows its own strength and resolve but has reasons to exaggerate them, so they can't trust what the other says.
2. **Commitment problems.** A deal today may not hold tomorrow, for example if one side is growing stronger and could break the deal later.
3. **Things that can't be split.** Some issues, like a holy site, are hard to divide. Fearon thinks this one is less important than the other two.

**Why it matters:** This paper set the framework most later research on war uses. Several other papers on this list build on it.

## 5. Gartzke (2007), "The Capitalist Peace"

- **Published in:** *American Journal of Political Science*, Vol. 51, No. 1
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Statistical analysis (wars, MIDs, and deadly disputes)

**What it's about:** A well-known finding is that democracies rarely fight each other (the "democratic peace"). Gartzke argues the real cause might be capitalism, not democracy.

**Main argument:** Three features of developed market economies reduce conflict:
1. **Wealth comes from ideas and capital, not land.** That makes conquering territory less worthwhile.
2. **Shared goals.** Since WWII, rich countries have mostly wanted similar things.
3. **Linked financial markets.** These give countries a way to signal and compete without fighting.

**Main finding:** Once you account for these economic factors, the effect usually credited to democracy mostly disappears.

## 6. Kinsella & Russett (2002), "Conflict Emergence and Escalation in Interactive International Dyads"

- **Published in:** *The Journal of Politics*, Vol. 64, No. 4
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Statistical analysis of country-pair interactions, 1951–1992

**What it's about:** Most research looks at military disputes. This paper also looks at lower-level hostility, like diplomatic spats, and asks whether the same factors drive both.

**Main findings:**
- All countries face competitive pressures that make disputes likely. But democracy and economic ties help stop those disputes from turning into war.
- Geography (being neighbors, for example) matters most in the early, low-level stages.
- Factors about *willingness* to fight matter more as a dispute escalates.
- They found no support for the idea that democracies pick diplomatic fights on purpose to signal resolve.

## 7. Quek (2016), "Are Costly Signals More Credible? Evidence of Sender-Receiver Gaps"

- **Published in:** *The Journal of Politics*, Vol. 78, No. 3
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Three controlled lab experiments (run at MIT)

**What it's about:** Theory says that spending money on a signal, like sending troops abroad, makes it believable. But it's hard to test this with real history because too many other things are happening at once.

**What he did:** Put people in lab experiments simulating a deterrence standoff. Some were randomly told they cared a lot about the issue.

**Main finding:**
- People who cared more *were* more willing to pay for costly signals, as the theory predicts.
- But the people receiving the signals often *didn't back off*, even though the sender had paid a real cost.
- He calls this a **sender-receiver gap**: the sender thinks the message is clear, but the receiver doesn't read it that way.

## 8. Slantchev (2005), "Military Coercion in Interstate Crises"

- **Published in:** *American Political Science Review*, Vol. 99, No. 4
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Game theory model (no real-world data)

**What it's about:** Earlier work treated military mobilization as just a sunk cost (money spent no matter what). Slantchev points out it does two things at once:
- **Sinks costs:** you pay for it whatever happens.
- **Ties hands:** it makes you more likely to win if war breaks out, which makes fighting more attractive.

**Main findings:**
- Once you include both effects, **bluffing becomes possible**, which earlier models ruled out.
- Peaceful outcomes can involve *more* military spending than ones that end in war.
- Military moves can create commitments, not just reveal existing ones. Getting the other side to understand them is still hard.

## 9. Tang (2026), "The Empirical Evidence of Audience Costs Theory: A Quasi-Meta-Analysis of Case Evidence Based on Bayesian Logic"

- **Published in:** *European Journal of International Relations* (2026)
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Review and re-scoring of existing case studies (a "quasi-meta-analysis")

**What it's about:** It looks back at about 30 years of research on Fearon's audience costs idea (#3) and asks how strong the real-world evidence actually is.

**What he did:** Collected historical case studies from many authors and scored how much each one should raise or lower our confidence in the theory. This uses Bayesian logic, which means updating your beliefs step by step as new evidence comes in.

**Main findings:**
- Evidence *for* the theory is thin. Evidence *against* it is fairly strong.
- Audience costs do exist, but they are smaller and depend more on the situation than supporters claim.
- Domestic politics affects foreign policy in many other ways too. Researchers should stop treating audience costs as the main link.

**Note:** This is the newest source here. Because it came out recently, other researchers haven't had much time to respond to it yet.

## 10. Weeks (2008), "Autocratic Audience Costs: Regime Type and Signaling Resolve"

- **Published in:** *International Organization*, Vol. 62, No. 1
- **Peer reviewed:** Yes
- **Type of study:** Theory (logical argument) + statistical analysis

**What it's about:** The standard view, from Fearon (1994), says democracies make more believable threats because their leaders can be punished by voters. Weeks challenges this.

**Main argument:** Many non-democratic leaders can also be punished, not by voters but by elites such as party officials or military leaders. Audience costs depend on three things:
1. Whether insiders can coordinate to punish the leader
2. Whether they see backing down as a bad thing
3. Whether foreign countries can see that this punishment is possible

**Main finding:** Democracies do **not** have a clear signaling advantage over most autocracies. The exceptions are personalist dictatorships (where one person controls everything and nobody can hold them accountable) and some monarchies.

## How the Papers Connect

- **The foundation:** Fearon (1995) asks why rational countries fight at all. Fearon (1994) offers audience costs as one way to make threats believable.
- **Challenging audience costs:** Weeks (2008) says autocrats can have them too. Tang (2026) says the real-world evidence is weak overall.
- **Other kinds of costly signals:** Slantchev (2005) says military moves both sink costs and tie hands. Quek (2016) finds costly signals don't always get through.
- **What drives escalation:** Carlson (1995) points to how much cost each side can tolerate. Braithwaite & Lemke (2011) find territory is the one reliable factor. Kinsella & Russett (2002) separate early-stage from late-stage causes.
- **Democracy vs. other explanations:** Gartzke (2007) argues capitalism explains the "democratic peace" better than democracy does.
